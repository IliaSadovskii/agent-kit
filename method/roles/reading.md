# Reading: what the owner just said, against what is written down

Somebody sat down and talked about their product. Their words are enclosed above, with a
number on every line. What this project has written down about itself is enclosed too, as
an index. Your work is to put the two together — and nothing else. You write no file, you
open no branch, you change nothing.

**Sort what was said, do not tidy it.** They talked in whatever order it came to them.
That is the point of the hour, and re-ordering it into something neat is where the details
that matter get lost.

Do this, in this order:

1. **Read every part the index lists.** Not the ones the telling seems to be about — every
   one. The cheap way to look thorough is to read a third of them and report three
   differences confidently, and that is exactly what a line per part cannot be faked into.

2. **Give each of them a line, and one of four verdicts.**
   - `unchanged` — the telling did not touch this part. Say so. This is work, not filler:
     it is the row that proves the reading was a reading. A part that is unchanged needs
     its `key` and nothing else.
   - `refines` — the telling says more about a part that is already written down, and
     nothing in it denies what stands. Return the part as it should now read.
   - `contradicts` — the telling says something the written part denies. Return the part
     as the telling would have it, **and a question**: one line the owner can answer,
     naming what is written down and what they just said. This is the only thing that goes
     to the person, so it has to stand on its own.
   - `new` — the telling adds a part this product has not written down. Leave `key` out;
     the program derives one.

3. **Sort the rest into the ledger.** There are three destinations and one test between
   them. *This changes what the product must do* — that is a part, above. *It does what it
   should and does it badly* — the ledger, `badly`. *It does not work at all* — the ledger,
   `broken`. Anything that is neither is not a thing they told you.

4. **Point at what they said.** Every line you return that is not `unchanged` carries
   `said`: the lines of the telling it comes from, as `L12` or `L12-L14`. The program
   resolves it against what they typed. There is nothing to quote and nothing to retype —
   and there is no way to point at something that was never said.

**Walk the product, not the slots.** Nobody holds their product as actors and entities.
They hold it as sign-in, the lesson, the account, the invoice. Those are what the parts
are, and their names are the owner's words — not the kit's, and not a translation of them.

**A part is one line's worth.** `name` is what it is called; `says` is what it is, in a
sentence somebody could read a month from now. What needs a paragraph is two parts.

**Do not invent.** If they did not say it, there is no line for it. A part worked out from
the code is a real thing and it is not yours: the step that reads code and writes down what
it found is the audit, and it does not exist yet. Here, everything comes from the telling.
